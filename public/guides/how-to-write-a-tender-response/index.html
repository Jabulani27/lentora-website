How to Write a Winning Tender Response

Bidding · 7 June 2026

A good tender response isn't about flair — it's about answering precisely what's asked, in the way the buyer will score it. Once you understand how evaluation works, writing a strong bid becomes a methodical task rather than a creative gamble.

At a glance

  • Everything you need is in the tender notice and associated documents: the specification, award criteria and assessment methodology.
  • Under the Procurement Act 2023, these documents must be provided free of charge and electronically.
  • Buyers score your bid against published award criteria — so structure your answer around them.
  • Method statements should be specific, evidenced and tied to this contract.
  • The biggest, most common mistake is answering the question you'd like, not the one that's asked.

Start with the documents

Before writing a word, read the associated tender documents in full. They contain the specification (what the buyer needs), the award criteria (what you'll be scored on), the assessment methodology (how scoring works), the contract terms, and the response and pricing schedules. Under the Procurement Act 2023, buyers must give you these free and electronically — so there's no excuse not to know exactly what's being evaluated.

Make a checklist of every requirement and every question. Your response has to address all of them.

Answer the award criteria — exactly

Buyers assess tenders against award criteria, using a published assessment methodology (often a scoring matrix). This is the single most important thing to understand: you are scored on the criteria, not on how impressive your bid sounds.

So:

  • Map your response to the criteria, point by point. If a question has four parts, answer all four.
  • Mirror the language of the question, so the evaluator can clearly see where you've answered.
  • Cover everything weighted. A brilliant answer to a low-weight question won't make up for a weak answer to a high-weight one.

(For how scoring works in detail, see how bids are scored.)

Write strong method statements

A method statement explains how you'll deliver. Weak ones are vague and generic; strong ones are specific and evidenced:

  • Be concrete. "We'll assign a named project manager and hold weekly progress reviews" beats "we have excellent project management."
  • Evidence your claims. Reference relevant experience, accreditations, or examples that show you've done this before.
  • Tie it to this contract. Buyers can tell a recycled answer. Reflect their specific requirements, location and priorities.
  • Show how you'll manage risk and quality, not just the happy path.

Don't forget social value and pricing

  • Social value carries real weight in public contracts — answer the specific themes asked, with measurable commitments (see what is social value).
  • Price clearly and completely. Fill in the pricing schedule exactly as required; errors or omissions here can sink an otherwise strong bid.

Before you submit

  • Re-read every question and confirm you've answered it in full.
  • Check the word counts and format requirements — overruns can be discounted.
  • Submit ahead of the deadline. Portals get busy; late bids are usually excluded automatically.

None of this is difficult in isolation. What defeats most small suppliers is that all of it has to happen at once, inside a three-week window, while the business still needs running. If you have started tender responses and abandoned them, that is usually the reason — not a lack of capability.

Frequently asked questions

What goes into a tender response?

Answers to the buyer's questions mapped to the award criteria, method statements explaining how you'll deliver, a completed pricing schedule, and any required declarations — all set out in the associated tender documents.

What is a method statement?

A written explanation of how you'll deliver the contract. Strong method statements are specific, evidenced and tailored to the particular requirement, rather than generic.

How are tender responses judged?

Against published award criteria, using a stated assessment methodology. You're scored on how well you meet the criteria, not on general impressiveness.

What's the most common mistake?

Answering generally instead of addressing the exact question and criteria. Map your response point-by-point to what's asked and weighted.
